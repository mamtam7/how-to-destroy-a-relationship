# Chapter 1: The Unilateral Decision
## *How to make a partnership a solo act*

---

> *"We have the Prius / I acted alone on that. We have the 5th wheel / I acted alone on that. We have three homes / I acted alone on that. I am saying you trusted me with all of that all along."*
> — Priya, January 7

---

## The Method

A partnership is, by definition, an agreement to make decisions together. To destroy it, you simply need to make those decisions alone, and then explain them later.

This is the method of the Unilateral Decision. It is highly effective because it doesn't require a fight or a confrontation. It is an act of simple bypass. You do not ask, "Should we do this?" You do not say, "We need to talk about this choice." You make the choice, execute it, and then present the result as a completed reality.

When the other person objects, you have several powerful moves at your disposal:

1. **Reframe exclusion as trust:** You explain that you made the decision because they "trusted" you, converting your refusal to consult them into a compliment.
2. **Offer the veto that isn't:** You ask for their input after the money has been spent, the papers signed, or the move completed—a gesture of consultation that is practically meaningless.
3. **Minimize the choice:** You frame the decision as minor, procedural, or simply "going with the flow," even when it involves significant financial or structural changes.
4. **Attack their insecurity:** You suggest that their desire to be consulted is not about partnership, but about their own "insecurity," "control issues," or "need for attention."

Over time, this behaves like a slow erosion. The excluded partner stops expecting to be consulted. They retreat into a corner of their own home, watching major life decisions sweep past them like weather events they can neither predict nor influence. They become a tenant in their own life, while the other person acts as the sole proprietor.

## In Practice

The Prius, the recreational vehicle, and three distinct homes—including the primary residence—were acquired and arranged through this method. On a single afternoon during a routine argument, Priya summarized this history not as a series of boundary violations, but as a defense:

> **Priya:** *"We have the Prius / I acted alone on that."*
> **Priya:** *"We have the 5th wheel / I acted alone on that."*
> **Priya:** *"We have three homes / I acted alone on that."*
> **Priya:** *"I am saying you trusted me with all of that all along."*

Marcus’s response was immediate and focused on the core issue:

> **Marcus:** *"Okay, so what you're saying is that there's no need for you to discuss anything with me."*

But the pattern had already been set years earlier. It began with smaller, everyday life decisions that were announced rather than discussed. 

In January 2022, immediately after the birth of their son, the choice of when to return to full-time work was delivered as an announcement, not a conversation:

> **Marcus:** *"We can discuss."*
> **Priya:** *"There is nothing to discuss. I am letting you know."*

---

By December 2022, this dynamic had expanded to real estate. Marcus received a text message containing the specific details of a property transaction:

> **Priya:** *"Lot 7 Plan 3 Includes ADU: $1,049,390. It won't be highest cost and we can add California room and solar."*

There was no negotiation or preliminary discussion recorded in the messages about whether they could afford the property, whether Marcus wanted to live there, or what the financial commitment would look like. It was a status update.

When the move finally occurred in August 2023, and Marcus raised questions about the speed and structure of the process, the response made his optional status explicit:

> **Priya:** *"The move will happen with or without you. We all want to feel important but guess what life continues with or without one."*

Marcus, seeking peace, accepted the premise:

> **Marcus:** *"Look, we got the house because of you. I know that."*

---

This bypass was not limited to investments. It extended to the composition of their daily living space. Family members and a mutual friend were moved into the household permanently without Marcus's structural agreement or a collaborative conversation. 

When Marcus attempted to name his discomfort with how the household was operating, the feedback shifted from decision-making to character analysis:

> **Marcus:** *"Why do I not have a say in how our household functions?"*
> **Marcus:** *"I'm a second class citizen in my own home and I don't have a say in how any of it operates."*

> **Priya:** *"I prefer you out on weekends as there is peace in the heart, house and mind."*
> **Priya:** *"You had nothing to do this morning so you picked a topic, made it an issue and brought your insecurity to it."*

Two days later, Marcus’s messages capture the resignation of a partner who has been systematically trained to stop asking for a voice:

> **Marcus:** *"I should be peaceful and harmonious no matter what decisions take place in my household, with or without my input."*
> **Marcus:** *"I still have a hard time accepting the fact that my input was completely ignored and that I'm now forced to share my space... Btw, your parents fully thought that you discussed with me and that we were on same page."*

---

The logical destination of this pattern is the ultimate unilateral decision: the dissolution of the relationship and custody of the child, handled with the same executive authority that purchased the Prius. 

In July 2026, the final terms were declared by Prius-method rules:

> **Priya:** *"You get the Edith house and everything else is mine. This is my final decision and I take our son for a year."*

And when Marcus protested, the declaration was simply repeated, accompanied by an insistence on agreement:

> **Priya:** *"My decision is final around that as well. I am glad we are on same page. It brings me peace!"*
> **Priya:** *"Let me know your final decision."*

And days later, the same absolute authority arranged the child's entire future geography over text:

> **Priya:** *"I will move away and give you our son."*
> **Priya:** *"I will still move to Oakland. I feel bad for our son but you can visit with him."*

## The Psychology

### The Erosion of Relational Efficacy

In family systems theory and relationship psychology, a central component of healthy functioning is *relational efficacy*—the belief by both partners that they have a voice, that their input can influence outcomes, and that they are active co-creators of their shared life. 

When one partner systematically makes major decisions alone, they crush this efficacy. The excluded partner experiences a psychological state known as *learned helplessness* (Martin Seligman). After repeatedly discovering that their opinions, concerns, and objections have zero impact on major life events (where they live, what assets they buy, who lives in their home), they stop trying. They learn that effort is futile.

This retreat is often mistaken by the making-partner as "peace," "harmony," or "trust." In reality, it is the silent shutdown of the excluded partner’s investment in the relationship.

### Power Imbalances and Demoralization

Research on the sociodemographic and interpersonal power structures of relationships (e.g., Blood & Wolfe; Avellar & Smock) indicates that when power imbalances become severe, they lead to profound demoralization. 

When a partner is told, either explicitly ("I don't care about your opinion") or structurally ("I prefer you out on weekends"), that their position is irrelevant, they undergo a form of marital exile. They are relegated to the status of a dependent or a bystander. 

The defense Priya offered—*"I am saying you trusted me with all of that all along"*—is a classic example of cognitive reframing used to manage guilt. It converts a refusal to collaborate (which is a boundary violation) into a sign of the partner's faith. It allows the unilateral actor to maintain a positive self-image ("I am a highly competent provider") while shielding them from the reality of the damage they have caused ("I am systematically excluding my spouse").

### Pathologizing the Complaint

To maintain a unilateral decision-making structure, you must have a way to neutralize the other person’s protests. If you acknowledge their protest as valid, you have to stop acting unilaterally. 

Therefore, you must pathologize the complaint. 

When Marcus protested his exclusion, Priya did not argue the merits of the decisions. She argued the merits of his complaining: *“You confirmed your insecurity,”* and *“You had nothing to do this morning so you picked a topic.”* This shifts the focus from the structural exclusion (which is real and documented) to Marcus's internal psychology (which is subjective and easy to attack). The complaint is no longer about the household—it is about Marcus's personal defects.

## The Damage Report

The Unilateral Decision destroys a relationship by turning a shared life into a hostile occupation. 

- **It kills safety.** To feel safe in a relationship, a person must know that their future cannot be overnight re-routed without their consent. When a house can be bought, a family moved in, or a vehicle acquired without discussion, the ground beneath the excluded partner is permanently unstable.
- **It breeds profound resentment.** The excluded partner carries a quiet, compounding anger. They feel used—as an accessory, a funding mechanism, or a co-signer, but never a partner.
- **It dismantles shared reality.** A couple that does not decide together does not live together. They live parallel lives inside the same coordinates, one acting as the director, the other as an extra.
- **It guarantees an asymmetric end.** The final, unilateral custody declarations ("I take our son for a year", "I will still move to Oakland") are not a sudden break from previous behavior; they are the natural culmination of a relationship that was run as a sole proprietorship from the start.

---

## The Irony

The person acting unilaterally almost always believes they are the competent one.

They see themselves as energetic, decisive, and capable. They believe they are doing the hard work of making things happen while the other person is passive, hesitant, or bogged down by "insecurity." They tell themselves, *“If I didn't make these decisions, nothing would get done.”*

They fail to see that they have created the very passivity they despise. 

By running ahead and acting alone, they have made it unsafe and useless for the other person to step forward. They have trained their partner to be a bystander, and then use that bystandership as evidence that they *must* continue to act alone.

They believe they are carrying the relationship on their back. What they are actually doing is carrying it to its grave.
